# The Eight Pillars, and the Shape of a Campaign

---

## 1. The pillars

Eight development axes that exist from 4000 BCE to the present. They do not unlock and
they do not complete — **each one changes form era by era but never goes away.** That is
what makes them different from a tech tree, and what lets one progression system span six
thousand years.

Two of the eight needed interpretation, and the readings are flagged so they can be
corrected:

> **"IT" is read as *information technology in the literal sense* — the technology of
> storing and moving knowledge.** On that reading it spans the whole game, because it
> genuinely does: the Vedic mnemonic schemes (*padapatha*, *kramapatha*, *ghanapatha*)
> are error-correcting codes, and among the most sophisticated pre-modern information
> technology anywhere. Not a modern anachronism dropped into an ancient setting — the
> oldest pillar of the eight.
>
> **"Cultivation" is read as cultivation *of people*** — schooling, apprenticeship,
> ordination, discipline — as distinct from **Agriculture**, which grows food. Both were
> listed, so they must differ, and this reading makes the set coherent.

| Pillar | What it is | 4000 BCE | 1000 CE | 1900 CE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Design** | Craft and form | Bead-drilling, pottery | Temple sculpture, textiles | Industrial design, print |
| **IT** | Storing and moving knowledge | Oral mnemonics | Palm leaf, Brahmi descendants | Print, telegraph, photography |
| **Structure** | Building and engineering | Tanks, embankments | Temples, step-wells, forts | Canals, rail, bridges |
| **Classicism** | Turning practice into *shastra* | — | Grammar, mathematics, medicine, poetics | Universities, disciplines |
| **Networking** | Connective tissue | Kin and clan | Guru lineages, guilds, pilgrimage, monastic circuits | Post, rail, press, congress |
| **Trade** | Exchange | Barter | Grain standard, coin, guilds | Cash economy, markets |
| **Cultivation** | Growing people | Apprenticeship | Gurukula, vihara, madrasa | Schools, colleges |
| **Agriculture** | Growing food | Foraging → domestication | Irrigation, double-cropping | Plantation, canal colony |

**Every work in the corpus feeds one to four pillars**, which is how the knowledge economy
drives development rather than sitting beside it. From the current 89-work corpus:

| Pillar | Works feeding it |
|---|---:|
| IT | 38 |
| Cultivation | 34 |
| Classicism | 33 |
| Networking | 28 |
| Design | 22 |
| Structure | 12 |
| Trade | 8 |
| Agriculture | 6 |

That distribution is itself a design signal. Agriculture and Trade are thin — which is
partly true of what survives (treatises on ploughing were less often recopied than
treatises on grammar) and partly a gap in the first cut. Both need filling: *Krishi-
Parashara*, *Vrikshayurveda*, guild records, the *Ain-i-Akbari*'s revenue tables.

**Industrialisation is not a pillar.** It is a late transformation *of* Structure, Design
and Trade, arriving in the 1850s — five and a half thousand years into the campaign, and
occupying the last 3% of it. The brief is right that it comes much later; the pillars are
what carry the other 97%.

## 2. The campaign

**You pick a region and you keep it.** Not a nation — a region: a river valley, a coast,
a plateau. You develop it across six thousand years while the rest of India runs its
pre-routed history around you, Victoria 3–style.

### What you can do

- Build, cultivate, endow, copy, teach, trade.
- Extract tribute; place your people; hold overlordship over neighbours (the sovereignty
  stack — [`04-eras.md`](04-eras.md) §3 — is the mechanism).
- Found colonies, in the historical Indian sense: settling and endowing a new place,
  which is what *agrahara* grants, monastic foundations and merchant quarters actually
  were.

### What you cannot do

**Leave.** The whole campaign is inside the subcontinent. You may trade across the Indian
Ocean — Rome, the Gulf, Srivijaya, China are all real markets that matter enormously —
but you cannot conquer outside India. This is a hard constraint and a good one: it makes
the game about **depth in one place** rather than breadth across a globe, which is the
opposite of every other grand strategy game, and is the whole point.

The consequence is that **your neighbours are not conquest targets, they are trading
partners you need to be strong.** A prosperous Bengal is worth more to a Tamil player
than a conquered one. That inverts the genre's default incentive, and it is what makes
the next section work.

### The invasions

India is invaded repeatedly, and the schedule is pre-routed and visible:

Achaemenid (518 BCE) · Alexander (327 BCE) · Indo-Greek and Saka (2nd c. BCE) · Kushan
(1st c. CE) · Alchon Huna (470s) · Sindh (712) · Ghaznavid (1000–1027) · Ghurid (1192) ·
Mongol raids (13th–14th c.) · Timur (1398) · Babur (1526) · Nadir Shah (1739) · Abdali
(1748–67) · European colonisation (1498 → 1757 → 1858).

**You defend your region — and you can defend other regions.** Since you cannot conquer
your neighbours and you profit from their strength, mutual defence is the rational play
rather than an altruistic one. That is a genuinely unusual strategic structure, and it is
the mechanical expression of the brief's "protect your state plus the other states."

Every invasion carries a **corpus risk** alongside its territorial one, and that is where
the two systems fuse: 1193 is not primarily a military event in this game. It is the year
Nalanda's library burns, and whether that matters depends entirely on what you copied out
in the preceding two centuries.

### The goal

Not conquest. **Arrive in the modern world with your country intact.** Scored on four
things:

1. **Corpus survival** — how much of the knowledge that existed still exists, and how
   much of it is *here* rather than in a European collection.
2. **Development** — the eight pillars across your region.
3. **The union** — how much of India arrives whole, developed and un-colonised, not just
   your own patch.
4. **Continuity** — whether the lineages are still alive, or whether you preserved texts
   while letting the traditions that read them die.

That fourth one is the sharpest, and it is the one a player will only understand on a
second playthrough.

## 3. Why this teaches history without being a lecture

The brief asks for a game that teaches while you play. The failure mode is obvious —
worthy, dull, a museum with a score attached. The design avoids it structurally rather
than by trying harder:

**The history is load-bearing.** You read *Lilavati* because knowing it lets you
commission derivations. You learn what was at Nalanda because you have to choose what to
copy out, and you cannot copy everything. You discover the *Yuktibhasha* was written in
Malayalam and stayed in Kerala because your networking pillar was too thin to carry it —
and you feel that as a loss, not as a fact.

**The lessons the structure teaches on its own**, without a single line of exposition:

- Neglect destroys more than invasion does. The 300-year recopying drain will cost you
  more works than every raid combined, and nobody will tell you that — you will notice it
  in your own grey ledger.
- Redundancy across borders is why anything survives. Players will independently
  rediscover that sending copies to Tibet was the best investment anyone made.
- Foundational work compounds. Patronising a grammarian in 400 BCE still pays in 1600 CE.
- Destruction and transmission happen in the same decades, by the same people. The corpus
  data enforces this ([`05-knowledge-economy.md`](05-knowledge-economy.md) §5).

## 4. Two risks worth naming now

**This game will be read politically.** A game about Indian civilisation, invasion,
library destruction and colonisation lands in the middle of a live and bitter argument in
contemporary India, whatever it does. It cannot avoid that by being vague; vagueness reads
as evasion and satisfies nobody. The only workable posture is to be **specific, sourced
and complete**: cite in-game, present contested scholarship as contested, and model the
whole record — including the Sultanate and Mughal patronage that produced Dara Shikoh and
Amir Khusrau, and including Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* as a challenge to the
corpus from inside it. A historian and a religious-studies reviewer belong on the team
from P1, not at certification.

**The scope has moved.** The knowledge economy is not a feature on top of the map game —
it is a full game in itself, and it is the differentiated part. Nothing else in the genre
has it, while every competitor has a map. **So the vertical slice should change**: build
the knowledge economy first, on a small map, rather than the map first with knowledge
bolted on later. Concretely, P0 becomes:

> One region, 800 BCE → 1250 CE. Twelve works. Reciters and scribes as pops. Grain
> patronage. The 300-year recopying drain. One catastrophe — 1193 — with the player able
> to see it coming for two centuries and able to copy things out. Map at L2–L6 only, no
> city dive.
>
> **If watching your own ledger go grey doesn't land, nothing else in this design
> matters.** That is worth finding out in eight weeks, before a single street is drawn.

The seventeen-rung map, the city dive and the procedural ground are still the right plan
— they are simply no longer what P0 exists to prove.
