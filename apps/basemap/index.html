The Base Map

Terrain, rainfall and colour, generated from 103 control points and 17 typed regions

No image, no mesh, no DEM. The landform comes from control curves; the micro-texture is slope-scaled ridged noise layered under a two-scale hillshade — the same technique a cartographer uses, done in a shader instead of by hand. Drag to pan, scroll to zoom.

Where the texture comes from

1 · Landform

15 ridges, 7 swells, 7 river troughs. Gaussian falloff off each control curve, domain-warped so nothing reads as a capsule or a disc.

2 · Roughness

The coarse gradient decides how much detail a place gets. The Gangetic plain stays glassy; the Ghats, the Aravallis and Chota Nagpur roughen up. Texture follows slope — that is the whole trick.

3 · Micro-relief

Six octaves of warped ridged noise, amplitude scaled by roughness and by altitude. Ridged noise carves valleys instead of adding bumps, which is what makes it read as drainage.

4 · Two-scale shade

A broad hillshade for the landform reading, a 1-pixel hillshade for the grain, mixed 55/45. Single-scale shading is what makes generated relief look plastic.

5 · Hypsometric tint

Thirteen elevation stops, sage through tan and brown to snow. Neighbours take the same ramp desaturated 82% toward bone, so only India carries colour.

Coastline, rivers and lakes: Natural Earth 10m, public domain. The India outline is used only as a visual emphasis mask. Natural Earth's depiction of Jammu & Kashmir, Aksai Chin and Arunachal Pradesh does not necessarily match Survey of India, and this one layer must be replaced with SOI geometry before any India release — see docs/00-plan.md §5. The terrain itself carries no boundary at all. Orography is 103 authored control points, not a sampled DEM.
